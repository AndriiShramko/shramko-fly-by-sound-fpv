# Sensory substitution and training: learning to perceive space by sound, and how to build a course for flying a drone by ear

Research note 02 for ShramkoSonicFPV, an open-source browser FPV drone simulator (WebGPU, Web Audio, PlayCanvas, TypeScript) that blind and low-vision pilots can fly by sound alone. Written 2026-11-07. Every claim is tagged with its strength: MEASURED (a study or a specification gives the number), REPORTED (a credible source states it without a measurement, or I could read only a secondary summary), INFERRED (my own reasoning from the evidence, not a finding). This is not legal or medical advice.

## Summary

- Speed of learning (MEASURED): narrow tasks with feedback were learned in hours (EyeMusic 2-3 h, cane-level collision avoidance with Sound of Vision after 4 h), but fine skill took tens of hours (73 h for fine vOICe acuity, 40-60 h for near-expert echolocation) [1, 2, 3, 4].
- Abandonment is the larger risk: after 40 years most devices stay in laboratories; 18 of 75 BrainPort trial participants dropped out, and 4 of 6 Sound of Vision users chose their cane when both were offered [2, 5, 6]. Build the course as a game for an activity people want, with pleasant, switchable sound.
- Display (MEASURED and INFERRED): one continuous stream plus one event stream, at most two parameters each, separated by timbre and stereo position, events 300 ms apart; hazard pulses driven by time to collision; pitch for height must be taught and be invertible, because blind adults showed no pitch-up link in one test; carry "off course" as roughness on a consonant tone [7, 8, 9, 10, 11].
- Teaching: state each rule first, then active practice in short sessions on separate days, advance after two consecutive blocks at 90%; teach shapes by flying outlines, not by listening quizzes [4, 12, 13].
- Flight: sonification supported coarse attitude and recovery in blind and sighted pilots, not precise manual control, so start in a self-levelling mode with altitude hold and remove assists later [14, 15].
- Children: early-blind children hear pitch and timbre well but judge relative source positions poorly, so encode relative quantities by pitch, timbre and tempo and keep position coarse; use 10-15 min play bursts, co-play and child-chosen sounds (game training improved spatial scores in 44 children over 12 weeks) [16, 17, 18].
- Measurement and law: success rate, collisions, path deviation and unprompted return rate; NASA-TLX, SUS, UEQ-S and a Smileyometer with an Again-Again table, read by the app's voice; in Poland get a parent's consent for anyone under 18 (digital consent age 16), record no audio by default and sign a short agreement with the school (not legal advice) [19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24].
- Recommendation: a first session that merges calibration, hover and speed, then walls with single obstacles, tunnels, rings and optional shapes, each unlocked by a pass criterion; a three-session fast path to a first ring course is plausible but rests on thin evidence and must be piloted (see the curriculum).
- Gaps, listed as "Not found" in each section: no measured sonification of altitude or speed for blind pilots, no study of shape judgement over headphones, no children flying by sound, no validated questionnaire for blind children.

## 1. Sensory substitution: what training bought, and why devices get abandoned

| Device (channel) | Training | What was measured | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| The vOICe (camera image scanned left to right over 1-2 s; height = pitch, brightness = loudness [25]) | mean 73 h (2 h a week, several months) | 9 early or congenitally blind adults, Snellen-E test: acuity 20/200 to 20/600, 5 of 9 better than the WHO blindness line (20/400), 66 degree view, no zoom [3] | MEASURED |
| vOICe, object tasks | about 15 h | locating and recognising objects on a table, including new ones (Auvray et al. 2007, read only through the review [26]) | REPORTED |
| vOICe, learning by doing | 4 h a day in daily life for 21 days, versus tests only | blindfolded users who practised in daily life were faster and more accurate; users who never used it stayed at chance (13 people, 3 practised) [27] | MEASURED, small n |
| EyeMusic (notes on a pentatonic scale, instrument = colour) | 2-3 h | all participants decoded basic shape and colour; users rated the sound pleasant and tolerable for long use [1] | MEASURED, small n |
| Sound of Vision (audio plus vibration, 3.5 m range) | 8 h over 4 weeks (4 h virtual, 4 h real) | 6 low-vision or blind adults: after 4 h collisions in a hard course equalled the white cane, trials stayed slower; median collisions 6.25 to 3.50 [2] | MEASURED, n = 6 |
| BrainPort V100 (electrical pattern on the tongue) | 15-20 h in one week [28]; the FDA study added a year of home use | untrained users could not beat chance [28]; FDA study: 52 of 57 completers passed object recognition (92.2%), 52 of 75 enrolled (69.3%) [6] | MEASURED |
| Neosensory Buzz and VEST (vibration; built for deaf users) | 1 month at home | 3-choice sound identification up to 95%, mean 70%, improving over the month [29]; authors work for the vendor | MEASURED |

- Narrow tasks with feedback pay off in hours (EyeMusic 2-3 h, Sound of Vision 4 h to cane-level collision avoidance); fine "vision-like" acuity took about 73 h. A flying display is a narrow task. INFERRED from the rows above.
- Active practice beats passive listening: practising in daily life helped [27], and the design review says training must be active so that the sensation is attributed to the outside world [26] (REPORTED). Maidenbaum's group stresses that its best results came only after a large amount of dedicated training [30] (REPORTED).
- Pleasantness is a design goal, not decoration: EyeMusic was built on a pentatonic scale because earlier devices "often generate unpleasant sensations" [1] (REPORTED).
- A vibration channel, for the later vest: motors on the lower back needed at least 6 cm spacing to be told apart (over 80% correct), and sweeps in time beat purely spatial patterns [31] (MEASURED).
- Abandonment, what is known: more than 40 years after the first devices they are still commoner in laboratories than in daily life; the review names ergonomics (blocking the ears; bone conduction suggested), cognitive overload, cost and availability, quality of the learning phase, and psychosocial factors [5] (REPORTED, a review, not a measurement).
- Choice behaviour is the only direct sign: given both aids, 4 of 6 Sound of Vision users picked the cane; the two who picked the device had more residual vision and less cane experience [2] (MEASURED, n = 6). The FDA BrainPort study lost 18 of 75 participants [6]; the earlier BrainPort rehabilitation study went from 18 to 13 people at 12 months [28] (MEASURED).
- Not found: any measured long-term daily-use or abandonment rate for an audio device. The dropout figures above are the only ones I could open.
- Consequence for ShramkoSonicFPV (INFERRED): do not build a general camera-to-sound aid that must beat the white cane. Build one display for an activity people already want to do for fun, so that the reason to return is the game, not the rehabilitation.

## 2. Sound design for non-speech displays

Cue types and what the evidence says:

| Type | Result | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Earcons (abstract musical motifs), auditory icons (real-world sounds), spearcons (speech sped up until it stops sounding like speech) | In five menu experiments spearcons beat earcons, auditory icons and hybrids on speed, accuracy and learning rate, and were about as learnable as plain speech [32] | MEASURED |
| Several earcons at once | Identification falls as their number rises; a different timbre per earcon and a 300 ms onset-to-onset stagger raise it significantly [8] | MEASURED |
| Parameter-mapping sonification (data value to a sound parameter, which is what a flight display is) | Review of 179 papers: pitch is the most used parameter, horizontal quantities are mostly rendered by spatial position and vertical ones by pitch; only 30 of 495 mappings (6.1%) were tested objectively, and polarity was not examined [33] | REPORTED (review of design practice) |

Mapping polarity, the direction in which a quantity maps to a sound:

| Mapping | Evidence | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Height to pitch | In sighted adults pitch maps spontaneously to vertical position, size and spatial frequency [34]; the pairing depends on language (Dutch "high/low" versus Farsi "thin/thick") [35]. In 24 early, late and congenitally blind adults, pitch change showed no congruency with the direction of a touch movement, while sighted adults did; the authors warn that vOICe-type designs assumed this link without testing it with blind users [10] | MEASURED |
| Speed to tempo | Faster tempo means faster speed for almost all sighted and blind listeners [36] | MEASURED |
| Size to pitch | Split about evenly in both groups, so avoid it; blind and sighted polarities agree overall (r = 0.82) but differ on some pairs, and early-blind listeners flipped one pair [36] | MEASURED |
| Closeness to pulse rate | Rate inversely proportional to distance, 1-15 Hz, exponential, plus a final "ping": 9 severely impaired walkers made smaller turn and path errors than with a single ping; some found the ticking annoying and masking [37] | MEASURED, n = 9 |
| Time to collision | Looming warnings that signal the true time to collision gave 80-160 ms faster braking than other non-looming warnings; braking time grew steadily as the signalled time to collision went from early to true to late (20 drivers) [9]; looming sounds are heard as faster than receding ones [38] | MEASURED |

- Pulses or a continuous tone: car parking aids pulse faster as the obstacle nears and switch to a continuous tone under 30 cm [39] (REPORTED, one manual). A continuous exponential tick was more accurate than discrete scale notes on long straight approaches; the authors attribute this to notes arriving too far apart to make the walker slow down [37] (MEASURED result, REPORTED explanation). I found no controlled study of pulse versus continuous tone for parking aids.
- Complexity costs time: in VR aiming at invisible targets (24 sighted people) pitch alone, pitch plus tempo and binary pitch plus tempo gave equal accuracy; a three-parameter display (Shepard tone, beats, roughness) was slower (median 15.8 s against 10.9-13.3 s), rated more demanding (NASA-TLX 52 against 42) and less usable (SUS 49 against 77). Audio-only aiming was more accurate than vision but 7 to 10 times slower (median 1.5 s with vision) [7] (MEASURED, sighted only).
- Cacophony: timbre and spatial position are the simple tools for keeping streams apart; dynamic pitch is a poor separator because it already carries data [40] (REPORTED), and an assistive display must convey only vital information to avoid overload [26] (REPORTED). I found no measured limit on how many non-speech streams can be tracked during a motor task.
- Musical structure: EyeMusic used a pentatonic scale on natural instruments to be pleasant [1]; a rising C-major scale of eight steps plus a final ping guided turns better than a lone ping, but one blind walker disliked music-like cues and another said he had to focus on the sound [37] (MEASURED). Roughness (amplitude modulation at 30-150 Hz) is the acoustic mark of screams and artificial alarms, and rougher sounds were rated more alarming [11] (MEASURED). Liking for consonance is learned, not universal: Tsimane' listeners rated consonant and dissonant chords equally pleasant yet reacted to roughness as Westerners do [41] (MEASURED). The Bremen group reports that roughness and beating give an "innate" calm-to-urgent guidance cue [42] (REPORTED by the authors), yet the three-parameter display above was the slowest for novices [7]; no study I opened compares dissonance with simple pitch or tempo as an off-course signal for blind users.
- Design consequences (INFERRED): use pitch for height only after teaching it and offer an inverted setting; map tempo to speed; drive hazard pulses from time to collision, not distance; keep one continuous stream plus one event stream, each with its own timbre and stereo position, and stagger events by at least 300 ms; carry "off course" with roughness added to a consonant tone, not with wrong notes.

## 3. Conveying shape by sound

| Method | Evidence | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Scan an image left to right (vOICe, EyeMusic) | Naive blindfolded sighted users resolved letter orientation well above chance on a first test and improved on a second [43]. People given only the conversion rule and a few example sounds matched sounds to raised shapes above the 20% chance level, and improved over 5 days [12]. After about 1 h online plus 20 min on site, sighted users named and placed EyeMusic shapes far above chance and generalised to shapes they had not been trained on [44]. | MEASURED, small n |
| One moving source tracing the outline around the head | 13 expert listeners at the centre of a loudspeaker ring named circle, triangle or square (3 choices): circles and triangles were better perceived than squares, and a 4-speaker ring caused confusions, mostly at slow speed [45]. | REPORTED (author poster) |
| Own movement coupled to sound | 22 blindfolded adults identified four shapes played over a vertical loudspeaker array; they improved only after active audio-motor training (reproducing the movement with the arm while a sound source on the wrist followed it), not after passive listening (11 per group) [13] | MEASURED |
| Pitch or loudness contour as the outline | Listeners read contours as straight or curved lines, and the scaling decides: pitch on an ERB-rate scale and linear amplitude sounded straight, other scalings sounded exponential or logarithmic; tempo contours were not reliably told apart [46] | MEASURED |

- Teach the rule first: knowing the conversion rule was enough to be above chance before any practice [12, 43], and musical training correlated with acuity [43] (MEASURED). So the first lesson states each mapping in words and demonstrates it; drills come after (INFERRED).
- Practice must use the learner's own movement: only active training improved shape recognition [13] and active practice helped localisation [27]. In a drone simulator the equivalent is flying the outline of a ring or gate rather than listening to a recording of it (INFERRED).
- A single moving source around the head is a coarse shape channel. Experts needed a dense speaker ring for reliable circle, triangle and square judgements [45]; headphones with a generic head model are coarser (INFERRED, not measured). Do not teach "circle versus square" as a listening quiz; teach it as the path the pilot flies and the sound sequence of gates on that path (INFERRED).
- Perceptually scaled ramps matter: build any pitch or loudness "line" on an ERB or similar perceptual scale so that a steady approach sounds steady [46] (MEASURED); do not use tempo to draw a contour.
- Not found: any study of circle versus square versus cube identification with a single moving source over headphones; any study of tapping points on an object to learn its shape by sound (the closest is the audio-motor training above); success rates for three-dimensional solids by sound.

## 4. Echolocation training

- The best controlled study: 14 sighted and 12 blind adults aged 21-79 trained for 20 sessions of 2-3 h on separate days over 10 weeks (about 40-60 h): echo size discrimination, orientation of a surface, virtual maze navigation from binaural echo recordings, and 20-30 min of walking indoors and outdoors. Both groups improved on every measure, sometimes to the level of expert echolocators; neither age nor blindness limited the rate of learning or transfer to untrained tasks; a no-click control task showed the gain came from click use. At the 3-month follow-up all blind participants reported better mobility and 83% better independence and wellbeing (self-report) [4] (MEASURED, small groups).
- Sighted learners sometimes beat blind learners, which the authors attribute to age or binaural hearing, not to vision [4] (REPORTED explanation). A sighted pilot is therefore a fair stand-in for a first test of a training design, but not for the end state (INFERRED).
- What to borrow (INFERRED): many sessions on separate days rather than one long block; a virtual task that provably transfers to a real one; a difficulty rule tied to success (in the study the target distance grew after two consecutive sessions above 90% correct [4], which is a ready-made pass criterion); a control task and an untrained-task test to separate learning from habituation; an independence questionnaire at follow-up.
- Scale of effort: near-expert echo skill took about 40-60 h of structured practice, against 2-8 h for narrow substitution tasks in section 1. Flying is closer to the narrow case, so the course should target hours, not months (INFERRED).
- Not found: independent outcome data for Daniel Kish's World Access for the Blind courses (I found only the organisation's own descriptions, which I did not cite); a controlled study of a game that trains echolocation in blind children.

## 5. Audio-only and blind-accessible games

| Game | Cues and design | What players or studies reported | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Last of Us Part II (2020) | Optional cues for jumpable gaps, ledges, pickups, incoming melee, aiming at an enemy and landed shots, each switchable, with volume sliders and a glossary to replay every cue; Enhanced Listen Mode plays a cue at each scanned target and its pitch changes with the target's height relative to the player (range 10-30 m and scan time 1-5 s adjustable); a button turns the camera toward the story path; speech covers menus; one preset switches the whole vision set on [47] | A visually disabled reviewer called the cues the most innovative setting; consultant Ian Hamilton said sighted players also used auto-rotation and the sonar ping, a lesson in universal design; a totally blind consultant advised on narration, cues and navigation [48] | REPORTED |
| Forza Motorsport (2023) | Blind Driving Assists: extra cues for position and orientation on the track, corner approach and progress, needed deceleration and shift points; each cue set can be enabled alone with its own pitch and volume; previewable with descriptions in the menu; built as a feature set, not a mode, with a blind consultant over two years [49] | A legally blind player: the first race was "a cacophony", then he tuned per-cue pitch and volume and lowered engine and tyre sound; finished 21st, 4th and 1st in three races [50] | REPORTED, one player |
| Columbia RAD (CHI 2018) | A "sound slider" for speed and trajectory plus a turn indicator that announces corners well ahead; built to avoid audio overload [51] | 15 participants in two studies preferred it to Mach 1; one blind player matched casual sighted players' lap times and paths (I read the press release, not the paper) [51] | REPORTED |
| A Blind Legend (2015) | Binaural-style audio with headphones; a companion voice gives directions and repeats on demand; heartbeat for low health; the screen reader steps aside during play [52] | The AccessWorld reviewer praised the tutorial; faults were menu taps that did not register, a fight he could not pass, and a purchase message telling blind users to ask for help [52] | REPORTED |
| Zform audio game (2002) | Hum from the centre of each hallway as a beacon (turn until it is equal in both ears), artificial stereo pan on wall bumps to show impact angle, per-room footstep sounds as landmarks, narration that can be interrupted; DirectX HRTF was judged discouraging [53] | Developer's account | REPORTED |
| Bucharest quadcopter game (2022-23) | Gamepad quadcopter, spatial audio, 20 min sessions: after 3 sessions most of 3 blindfolded players were at ease with the sonification; spectral tuning cut ear fatigue, and busier scenes raised it; players beelined at the target sound and got stuck in mazes, so a path cue is needed; aeroplane-style controls were dropped because the player could not hold a heading for audio orientation [54]; a later paper describes the game and its sonification model [55] (abstract only; the publisher blocks automated access) | three blindfolded sighted players, 20 min sessions | MEASURED, n = 3 |

- What to copy (INFERRED): every cue switchable with its own volume and pitch plus a "calm" preset; a preview and glossary of every cue before play; one beacon sound that is steered until it is centred; a path cue and not only a target cue; bundles of assists that can be taken apart; features built for blind players that sighted players also use.
- The first-session failure mode is cacophony, not boredom: the Forza player's first race, Zform's "not so many that ears are overwhelmed" and Columbia's overload goal all point the same way [50, 51, 53] (REPORTED).
- Menus and speech: the Game Accessibility Guidelines ask for screen-reader support including menus, voiced text, separate volume for effects, speech and music, distinct sounds per object and event, binaural simulation and a pingable sonar-style audio map [56] (REPORTED). In a browser (design INFERRED from the MDN documentation [57]): ordinary DOM text with `aria-live="polite"` for status, the Web Speech `SpeechSynthesis` interface as a built-in voice for people without a screen reader, interruptible narration [53], and the Web Audio `HRTF` panner, which uses measured human impulse responses, while the default `equalpower` is simpler.
- Not found: documentation I could open for Top Speed, Mach 1 and Swamp (the audio-game database blocks automated access); any game called "Racing Blind"; any audio-only flying game with published cue design or player results, apart from the Bucharest work. Existence claims for Three-D Velocity and similar flight simulators came from search summaries only, so I do not cite them.

## 6. Blind people flying, and drone sonification

- Real aircraft: Miles Hilton-Barber had flight-instrument output converted to speech (angle of bank, altitude, track) and trained four years with a sighted co-pilot before his London to Sydney microlight flight; he still needed that co-pilot [58] (REPORTED, his own account). Blind pilots currently use an attitude sonification to control aircraft attitude [14] (REPORTED).
- Attitude by sound, simulator: blind and sighted pilots flying successive manoeuvres on sonification alone both reached target attitudes with good accuracy and did not differ significantly; harder manoeuvres raised subjective workload and lowered brain responses to unexpected sounds, so the authors restrict the display to simple recovery and guidance manoeuvres [14] (MEASURED).
- Attitude by sound, real aircraft (16 pilots, vision blocked): roll was an interaural level shift towards the high wing; pitch was a spectral tilt plus an added pitch of 1000 Hz nose-up and 2400 Hz nose-down, chosen over spatial elevation cues because elevation localises poorly without individual head models and in noise. Errors fell by about 40% (pitch) and 90% (roll) against no audio, the first recovery move was correct in about 85% of trials, 3 of 160 trials were aborted, and the authors say precise control was not expected. They also note that earlier displays made of plain tones and noise were fatiguing and annoying and pilots disengaged them, which is why they used the pilot's own music as the carrier [15] (MEASURED, REPORTED for the history).
- Drones and UAVs: continuous sonification of course deviation gave 1.9 s (19%) faster reactions than discrete alerts in 39 military participants, but it interfered with discrete late-arrival alerts [59] (MEASURED). A spatialised pulse sonification (shorter pulse period as the helicopter drifts to an obstacle) in cockpit noise was localised to between under 3 and 14 degrees, with front-back reversals; a narrowband tone with level differences beat a broadband tone with generic head-model spectral cues (4 participants, two of them authors) [60] (MEASURED, tiny n).
- Blind and low-vision drone pilots, interviews (59 survey answers, 13 interviews): 54% had high interest and 55% had never flown or been near a drone; the main motive was flying with sighted family. They want altitude, speed and direction, speech only for threats such as crash warnings, perceptual beeps and tones for 3D space, a user-controlled amount of feedback ("one brain and only two ears"), audio not from the noisy drone but through headphones (one asked for bone conduction), and a sighted instructor in the first flights because fear of a crash stops exploration [61] (REPORTED, small sample).

| Quantity | Cue with some evidence | Gap |
|---|---|---|
| Roll or bank | left-right level shift [15]; an attitude sonification used by blind pilots [14] | no test with FPV-style fast rotation (INFERRED gap) |
| Pitch angle | spectral tilt plus pitch [15] | nose-up got the lower tone, the reverse of "up = higher"; whether that was easy to learn is not reported |
| Altitude | none measured; users ask for it [61] | I found no study of altitude sonification with measured performance |
| Speed | tempo (polarity agreed by blind and sighted [36]); "sound slider" [51] | not tested for flight |
| Obstacle or wall | pulse period shrinks with closeness, spatialised [37, 60] | tested with 4 and 9 people |
| Off course | continuous sonified deviation [59] | masks discrete alerts |

- Not found: any published blind FPV racing pilot; a measured sonification of drone altitude or speed with blind users; the Neubauer and Teas 1992 Air Force report (the host blocked it) and the Sound Flyer's exact mapping (publisher blocked). EarPilot, an iPhone app that lets a blind pilot fly basic manoeuvres in an aeroplane, is described only in a conference talk blurb [62] (REPORTED, no results).
- Consequence (INFERRED): start flight training in a self-levelling mode with altitude hold, because the evidence supports coarse attitude and recovery by ear and not precise manual control; the Bucharest game also dropped aircraft-style controls for quadcopter-style translation [54]. Remove assists step by step.

## 7. Children and students

Early-blind versus late-blind hearing, which decides what to encode:

| Finding | Source | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Congenitally and early blind adults discriminate pitch and timbre better than matched sighted adults; late-blind adults do not | Wan et al., as summarised in a review [16] | REPORTED |
| Nine congenitally blind adults (27 sighted controls) were severely impaired at spatial bisection (is the middle of three sounds nearer the first or the third?): thresholds 4.2 times typical, half at chance; simpler spatial tasks and temporal bisection were intact | [63] | MEASURED |
| Congenitally blind children aged 9-14 show the same bisection deficit and a lower minimum-audible-angle precision, with temporal tasks intact; the authors say the capacity recovers with age | [17] | MEASURED |
| Early-blind people can be worse at judging vertical position and the relative position of three sounds, yet better at peripheral localisation and distance discrimination | background section of [4] | REPORTED |

- Design consequence (INFERRED): carry relative quantities (closer, faster, off course) with pitch, timbre and tempo, which early-blind listeners hear well, and keep spatial position coarse (left, centre, right) instead of asking for fine comparisons of source separation. Pre-test polarity per user, because blindness onset changed polarity in at least one mapping [36].
- Game training works for children: 44 visually impaired children aged 6-17 trained for 12 weeks with a wearable audio bracelet, in weekly 45 min sessions with a rehabilitator (9 h) plus about 5 h a week at home with a relative (60 h; 69 h in total); the child chose the sounds (elephant, mosquito, drums) to stay motivated; the audio group improved on almost all spatial tests and the control group, taught psychomotor lessons at other centres, did not (not randomised) [18] (MEASURED). The therapist or relative wore the device too, to make it more entertaining.
- A short game can transfer: seven early-blind 16-17 year olds explored an audio-only action-game model of a real building for 60 min (two 30 min sessions plus 15 min of familiarisation); route finding in the real building was markedly good and correlated with game score [64] (MEASURED, n = 7). Earlier, AudioDoom, a 3D acoustic story world, was field-tested with blind children in a Chilean school and spatial sound produced navigable mental structures (preliminary) [65] (REPORTED).
- Formats already used with visually impaired children: LEAP offers free open-source binaural games, tic-tac-toe in two-dimensional audio space, tennis for movement and speed in three-dimensional audio, and "Curve", where the child hears an equation's graph as notes and repeats it before a time limit [66] (REPORTED). Participants in a navigation training study treated it as a game and all enjoyed it, which the review links to less distress than real-world training [5] (REPORTED).
- Teachers and instructors: the instructor should observe, offer help when needed and tailor the programme, and users can be paired to help each other [5]; visually impaired adults asked for a hands-on sighted instructor in the first drone flights because fear of crashing stopped them exploring [61] (REPORTED).
- Session length: no study I opened reports an optimal length for children. Sessions ran 20 min in the Bucharest game, where fatigue rose with scene complexity [54], 30 min twice in Connors' game and 45 min weekly in Cappagli's. I therefore propose bursts of 10-15 min with a break and no more than about 30 min a sitting (INFERRED, to be tested).
- Not found: a controlled study of children flying or steering by sound; guidance from a teacher-of-the-blind body on audio-game session length; studies of audio-only games with blind children under six.

## 8. Measuring progress

Objective measures that earlier studies used, and what to add for flight:

| Measure | Used in | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Proportion correct, and the distance or difficulty reached | echolocation training: level raised after two sessions above 90% [4] | gives a pass rule |
| Collisions (major 1.0, minor 0.5), obstacles correctly reported before passing | Sound of Vision [2] | the authors call completion time not meaningful alone, because it must be read in context |
| Average time to collect a target, collisions per scene | Bucharest game [54] | trajectories were logged and plotted |
| Rotation error, rotation time, over-walking distance | turn guidance [37] | error shrinks with the pairing of tick and ping |
| Aiming error and aiming time | VR aiming [7] | accuracy and speed trade off |
| For flight (INFERRED) | gate pass rate, cross-track error from the path centre line, altitude error, collisions per minute, recovery time after a push, and the assist level at which success was reached | add transfer: success on a course never practised |

- Questionnaires. NASA-TLX has six scales (mental, physical and temporal demand, own performance, effort, frustration), weighted by 15 pairwise comparisons in the official procedure [19]; Apavou's audio study found 52 against 42 between the heavy and light display [7]. SUS is 10 items on a 5-point scale, item scores mean nothing alone, and the mean of 500 studies is 68 [20, 67]; Hoffmann used the 10 SUS items plus one leisure item with visually impaired users [2]. The UEQ has 26 items on a 7-point scale in six scales, takes 3-5 min, exists in 21 languages, and has an 8-item short form (UEQ-S) [21].
- Children: the Fun Toolkit (Smileyometer faces, Again-Again table with yes, maybe, no, Fun Sorter) was built for ages 5-10 [68]; the Smileyometer is best shown before and after the activity, and young children often give the top score to everything (more than 40% of younger children against under 10% of older ones in one study) [22] (REPORTED). I found no validated version for blind children; the faces are visual (INFERRED).
- Voice delivery (INFERRED design, with two measured hints): in a study with blind and low-vision participants a sighted assistant read the consent form and helped with the computer [36]; self-administered audio questionnaires (audio-CASI) raised reports of sensitive behaviour by factors of 3 or more over paper forms in 1,690 adolescent males, so the voice can replace an interviewer [69] (MEASURED, other topic). Build the questionnaire into the app: the voice reads each item with its number and verbal label, the answer is a key press or gamepad button, items repeat on request, and the whole set is at most 10 items. For the six TLX scales use a 0-10 scale and skip the weights (not validated; I did not open a validation).
- First-time testers: what each sound meant (a glossary quiz), which direction they expected "up" to be, annoyance and ear fatigue, fear of crashing, Smileyometer before and after, "would you play again?". Regular testers: workload (TLX), SUS and UEQ-S, which cues they switch off, whether they came back unprompted (the best measurable proxy for abandonment), and a yes-or-no follow-up on independence and wellbeing as in the echolocation study [4] (INFERRED from the sources above).
- Sample sizes: the median in accessibility papers with disabled participants is 13 [70] (MEASURED, literature survey). Report each person's learning curve and not only means; one of six Sound of Vision users progressed slowly and another found the system intuitive [2].
- Not found: a validated child-friendly questionnaire for blind children (a visuotactile satisfaction scale for children with visual impairment is listed in a 2024 journal but I could not open it); a published validation of voice-delivered TLX; the original paper on positive response bias in accessibility studies (Trewin et al., ASSETS 2015) could not be opened, so I do not rely on it.

## 9. Ethics and law for testing with minors in Poland and the EU

This is a practical outline, not legal advice; the school's data protection officer and a Polish lawyer should confirm it.

- Who is a child: in Poland anyone under 18 is a minor, and from 13 to 18 has limited capacity to act [23]. The EU data protection authorities' opinion on children and schools treats everyone under 18 as a child, puts the child's best interest first, says the data protection rights belong to the child and are only exercised by the representative, and says consent can grow from consulting the child to joint consent of child and representative to the child's own consent when mature [71] (REPORTED, official opinion).
- Consent age: under GDPR Article 8, for services offered directly to a child where consent is the legal basis, processing is lawful if the child is at least 16, otherwise only with consent or authorisation from the holder of parental responsibility, and the controller must make reasonable efforts to verify it; states may lower the age, not below 13 [24]. Poland has not lowered it, so it is 16 [23, 72]. No Polish method of verifying parental consent is prescribed, and if both parents hold parental authority each may act alone as the child's representative [23]. Practical rule: a signed consent from one parent or guardian for every participant under 18, plus the child's own age-appropriate assent, which matches the joint-consent idea in [71] (INFERRED).
- What is personal data here: any information relating to an identifiable person, which covers a voice recording and also play logs that can be tied to a child [24]. The European Data Protection Board states that voice data is inherently biometric personal data and that voice authentication gets stronger protection [73]. Visual-impairment status is data concerning health (Article 4), a special category that needs explicit consent (Article 9) [24].
- Minimise by design (INFERRED from the rules): no names, no audio and no video by default; random participant codes; log task events only; take notes by hand; delete on request. The rules behind this are data minimisation and storage limitation (Article 5), withdrawal of consent at any time and as easily as it was given (Article 7), information in clear plain language, "in particular" when addressed to a child (Article 12), and the statement that children merit specific protection (Recital 38) [24]. If a think-aloud session must be recorded, ask a separate explicit consent from parent and child, state the purpose, keep it short, never publish it, and use safeguards such as pseudonymisation, which Article 89 requires for research [24]. Polish law also needs a parent's consent before a child's image is disseminated [23]; treat video and voice alike.
- What a school should sign (INFERRED): the head teacher's written permission for the activity; a one-page cooperation agreement that says who decides what is collected, either as separate controllers or as joint controllers with an arrangement whose essence is shown to the data subjects (Article 26), and a processor contract if a party only hosts or stores data for another (Article 28) [24]; the information notice and the consent form, sent out by the school so that names stay with the school; a line that taking part is voluntary and has no effect on marks; and a contact for withdrawal and for complaints to the Polish authority UODO.
- Not found or not checked: Polish guidance specific to research with pupils (for example approval by the education authority); the official Polish act and the EU regulation texts, because the official sites block automated access (I used an unofficial copy of the GDPR articles); whether a claim to compensate for blindness would make the app a medical device under Regulation (EU) 2017/745 (avoid therapeutic claims until that is checked).

## Recommended curriculum

This is a design proposal (INFERRED from the evidence cited). The evidence covers basic competence on narrow tasks in hours; precise control and expertise took tens of hours or months [2, 3, 4]. Steps unlock by pass criteria, not by hours. All numbers in the pass column are proposals to be tuned in a pilot, except two rules that come from studies: "two consecutive blocks at 90%" from the echolocation study [4], and "80% azimuth and 70% elevation on two consecutive blocks" from a localisation training study whose gains lasted a month and reached untrained positions [16].

| Step | Teach | Pass criterion | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. What is that sound (10 min, merged into step 1) | headphone left-right check; each cue named by voice and previewed in a glossary; the user picks up = higher or up = lower tone; per-cue volume and pitch; a "calm" preset | names every cue in two consecutive blocks of 10 at 90% or better; polarity choice stays consistent in 8 of 10 trials | rule-first gave above-chance results before practice [12, 43]; blind listeners may not share "up = higher" [10]; previews and sliders [47, 49]; per-cue timbre [8] |
| 1. Hover and height (self-levelling on, altitude hold off) | height by pitch, heading by left-right balance, hold a band | in the band 80% of the time for 20 s, 4 of 5 attempts | coarse attitude control by sound works in blind and sighted pilots [14, 15]; users want altitude, speed, direction [61]; quadcopter-style controls [54] |
| 2. Move and stop (same session as 1) | speed by tempo; fly 30 m straight and stop in a zone | stop in the zone in 8 of 10 runs | tempo to speed polarity agreed by blind and sighted [36]; speed "slider" cue [51] |
| 3. Walls and single obstacles (one step, one cue) | pulse period shrinks with time to collision, spatialised, final ping | at most one touch in 8 of 10 approaches | pulse and ping beat a lone ping [37]; time to collision [9, 38]; cane-level collision avoidance after 4 h on a hard course [2]; localisation 3-14 degrees [60] |
| 4. Tunnels (two walls) | centre between two proximity streams, each with its own timbre and stereo slot, events 300 ms apart; a one-wall "follow" step first | 50 m tunnel with at most 2 touches in 3 of 4 runs | concurrent streams interfere unless timbre and onset differ [8]; a third parameter slowed aiming [7]; centre a beacon until it is equal in both ears [53] |
| 5. Rings | beacon hum from the ring centre, a "passed" earcon, a path cue to the next ring | 8 of 10 rings, then a 6-ring course without a collision | arrival ping raised accuracy [37]; players beeline and need a path cue [54]; early corner warning [51] |
| 6. Shapes (optional) | fly the outline of a circle, square or triangle made of rings; name it afterwards | complete it within a path-deviation limit set from pilot data | active practice beat passive listening [13]; moving-source shape judgement is coarse [45] |
| 7. Remove assists (stretch) | self-levelling off, altitude hold off, with a rescue assist | no fixed criterion | precise manual control by sound was not achieved [14, 15] |

- Skip or merge: merge steps 0, 1 and 2 into one first session; merge walls and single obstacles because they use the same cue; treat tunnels as the real bottleneck, since it is the first step with two simultaneous streams; skip listening quizzes of "circle versus square" and make shapes a flying exercise (evidence is thin); skip image-to-sound scanning and echo clicks, which cost tens of hours for skills the simulator does not need [3, 4].
- Faster path, with weak evidence: three sessions of about 20 min on separate days to a first ring course in self-levelling mode. Support: most of 3 blindfolded players were at ease after 3 sessions of 20 min [54]; 60 min of game play transferred to real navigation in 7 early-blind teenagers [64]; 2-3 h gave shape and colour decoding with EyeMusic [1]; 4 h reached cane-level collision counts [2]. Test it with 6-10 testers and publish each person's curve; fix a falsifiable target before the pilot (my placeholder: 70% of rings passed by session 3; this is not a finding).
- Game version for children (INFERRED from [5, 17, 18, 54, 61, 63, 64, 66]): a companion voice names each sound before it appears; the child picks the sound set; missions of 10-15 min each add one cue (find the music box, squeeze between two walls, fly through bubble rings, draw a star in the sky); no fail state, a crash is a soft bump and an automatic re-centre; stars for rings; a parent or teacher flies too on a second controller and listens; 2-3 short sessions a week; relative quantities (closer, faster, off course) by pitch, timbre and tempo and position kept to left, centre, right; ask "again?" with the Again-Again table read aloud; consent and data rules as in section 9.
